How to use the solar calculator

The calculator works out what size of solar system you need by looking at what you actually want to run and for how long. You do not need to know anything about solar to use it — you just need a rough idea of your appliances.

It takes about two minutes.

  1. Say where the system is going

    Choose Home, Office or Travel / RV. This just changes which appliances are offered first — the search box finds anything from any of the three, so pick whichever is closest.

  2. Tap the appliances you need to run

    Tap an appliance to add it to your list; tap it again to take it off. Each one you add appears in Your load list, where you can change how many you have, how many watts each one draws, and how many hours a day it runs.

    If something is missing, open Add an appliance that isn't listed and enter it yourself.

  3. Set your location and equipment

    Pick the region closest to you — this sets how much sun your panels can expect. Then say how many hours of grid or generator power you get each day, how long the batteries need to carry the load on their own, and what kind of batteries you plan to buy.

  4. Read your system

    The results update as you go. They tell you how many panels, how many batteries, and what size of inverter and charge controller you need. Use Print / save as PDF to keep a copy or take it to a supplier.

Getting the numbers right

Where do I find an appliance's wattage?

Look for a sticker or metal plate on the back or underside of the appliance, or inside the door. You want the number followed by W (watts). If it only shows amps and volts, multiply them together: 2A × 230V = 460W.

If you cannot find it, leave the figure the calculator suggests. Those are typical values for that kind of appliance and are close enough for planning.

Be honest about the hours

This is where most people go wrong. Hours per day means the time the appliance is actually switched on and working, averaged across a normal day. A TV that is on all evening is about 5 hours, not 24.

Don't forget the things that never switch off

Routers, decoders, CCTV cameras and standby lights each draw very little, but they draw it for 24 hours a day. Together they often add up to more than an appliance you use for an hour. Add them.

Fridges and freezers are a special case

Leave the hours at 24. A fridge is plugged in all day, but its compressor only runs about a third of the time — the calculator already accounts for this, which is what the small 35% duty label on the row means. If you reduce the hours yourself you will undersize the system.

Watch out for anything that makes heat or cold

Kettles, irons, heaters, ovens, hair dryers and air conditioners use far more power than everything else combined. Adding a single 1,500W kettle can double the size of a small system. They are still worth including if you genuinely need them — just expect the numbers to jump.

If your grid power comes and goes

In many places mains electricity is only available for part of the day. That changes the answer substantially, and the calculator handles it — set Mains or generator power to the hours you actually get.

Yes, the mains can charge your batteries too

A hybrid inverter/charger does two things at once while the power is on: it runs your appliances straight from the mains, and it refills the batteries ready for the next cut. Tick Charge the batteries too and the calculator will size the charger you need for that.

This makes the system considerably cheaper

If you get 8 hours of mains a day, the batteries only need to cover the other 16 — not a whole day. That is roughly a third off the most expensive part of the system. Tell the calculator about your mains hours and it will size for the gap instead of for total independence.

Check the recharge warning

Batteries can only be charged so fast. Lithium will take a lot of current; lead-acid types much less. If your mains window is short and your battery bank is large, there simply may not be enough time to refill it before the power goes off again — so you would start each day part-charged and slowly fall behind.

The calculator checks this and warns you. If you see that warning, your options are more solar to make up the difference, a smaller battery bank, or lithium batteries, which accept a much faster charge.

How much should come from solar?

Use Solar should supply to decide. Choosing Everything sizes the array to cover your whole day, with the mains there as insurance only. Choosing a smaller share means a smaller, cheaper array, with the grid making up the difference — sensible if your power is fairly reliable and you mainly want to cut the bill and ride out the cuts.

Understanding your results

The three big numbers

  • kWh per day — how much energy your appliances use in a day. This drives everything else.
  • Watts of panels — the total size of the solar array you need on the roof.
  • kWh of battery — how much storage you need to get through the night and cloudy spells.

Solar array

Minimum array is the calculated requirement; installed is what you actually get once you round up to whole panels. Expected harvest is roughly how much energy those panels will collect on an average day.

Battery bank

Nameplate energy is what the batteries are sold as. Usable energy is what you can safely take out of them. Those differ because draining a battery flat every day destroys it — the calculator sizes the bank so the usable figure covers your needs. The wiring line tells you how to connect them.

Inverter

The inverter turns battery power into normal mains power. The main figure is what it must supply continuously. Startup surge is the brief spike when a motor kicks in — fridges, pumps and air conditioners pull two to three times their normal draw for a second or two, and your inverter has to survive that.

If nothing you listed runs on mains power, the inverter shows zero — you do not need one.

Charge controller

This sits between the panels and the batteries and controls the charging. The number is the current rating you need, in amps.

Worth knowing

If a yellow-bordered box appears below the results, read it. It flags practical problems with the system you have just described — cable sizes that will be awkward, a surge your inverter may not handle, or an array too small to recharge the bank in a day.

Common questions

Why does it need more panels than my daily usage suggests?

Because nothing in the chain is perfect. Panels lose output to heat, dust and wiring; the charge controller loses a little more; and storing energy in a battery and taking it back out loses some too. The calculator adds roughly 30% to cover all of that. A system sized without that margin runs flat within a week.

Why did it choose 24V or 48V instead of 12V?

Higher voltage means less current for the same amount of power, and less current means thinner, cheaper, safer cable. Once your load gets past a certain size, staying at 12V would need very heavy cable and would waste a lot of energy as heat. You can force a particular voltage under Advanced options if your equipment requires it.

Which battery type should I choose?

Lithium (LiFePO4) costs more up front but you can use 80% of its capacity and it lasts several times longer, so it is usually cheaper over its life and needs a smaller bank for the same job.

Lead-acid types — tubular, AGM, flooded — are cheaper to buy but you can only use about half of their capacity, so you need roughly a 60% bigger bank, and they need replacing sooner. Flooded batteries also need topping up with water.

How many days of backup do I need?

One day is fine if you have grid power or a generator to fall back on. Choose two or three if the system is your only source of power, or if you get long overcast spells. More days means more batteries, which is the most expensive part of the system — so do not choose three unless you need it.

Can I run an air conditioner on solar?

Yes, but it will dominate the system. Add one and watch the numbers — an inverter-type AC uses roughly half the power of a standard one and is much kinder to a solar setup. If the result is more than you want to spend, running the AC only in the evening, or cooling one room instead of the whole house, makes a large difference.

What does the DC checkbox mean?

Most appliances run on mains power (AC). Some — mainly in vans, boats and off-grid cabins — run directly on 12V or 24V (DC) instead. DC appliances skip the inverter entirely, so they waste less energy. Only tick the box if you know the appliance runs directly off the battery.

Can the grid charge my batteries when it is on?

Yes, if you use a hybrid inverter/charger — that is exactly what it is for. While the mains is on it runs your appliances directly and charges the batteries at the same time; when the power cuts, it switches over to the batteries automatically, usually fast enough that you do not notice.

Set your mains hours in the calculator and tick Charge the batteries too. You will get the charger size you need, and a check on whether your mains window is long enough to actually refill the bank.

How accurate is the price estimate?

It is a guide to the order of magnitude, not a quote. Equipment prices vary a lot by brand, quality and where you buy. Use it to know whether you are looking at a few hundred or several thousand, then get real quotes.

Can I save or share my results?

Yes — use the Print / save as PDF button. In the print dialogue choose "Save as PDF" as the destination. The controls are removed automatically, so you get a clean summary of your load list and system.

Is anything I enter saved or sent anywhere?

No. Everything is worked out inside your own browser and nothing is stored. If you close the page, your list is gone — so print or save it if you want to keep it.

Glossary

Watt (W)
How fast an appliance uses power while it is running.
Watt-hour (Wh) and kilowatt-hour (kWh)
How much energy is used over time. A 100W bulb left on for 10 hours uses 1,000Wh, which is 1kWh. This is the unit your electricity bill uses.
Peak sun hours
Not daylight hours. It is the number of hours per day your panels would produce at their full rating — typically 3 to 6, depending on where you live.
Duty cycle
The share of the time a thermostat-controlled appliance is actually drawing power. A fridge is on all day but its compressor runs perhaps a third of it.
Depth of discharge
How much of a battery's capacity you can safely use. Lithium about 80%, lead-acid about 50%.
Surge
The brief spike in power when a motor starts. Fridges, pumps and air conditioners can draw three times their normal watts for a second or two.
MPPT and PWM
Two kinds of charge controller. MPPT extracts noticeably more energy from the same panels and is worth the extra cost on anything but the smallest systems.
Pure sine wave
The type of inverter that produces clean mains power. Modified sine wave is cheaper but can hum, run motors hot, and upset sensitive electronics and some medical devices.
AC and DC
AC is normal mains power from a wall socket. DC is what batteries and panels produce. The inverter converts DC to AC.

Before you buy

These results will get you to the right size of system and a realistic budget — enough to talk to suppliers without being sold something twice as big as you need.

They are not an installation design. Cable sizes, fusing, earthing, panel mounting and battery ventilation all need a qualified installer, and getting those wrong is a fire risk rather than an inconvenience. Take your printout to a professional and have them confirm it.
